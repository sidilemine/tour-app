By 1870, trains from Finchley's Church End reached London in half an hour. The older world of horse-drawn travel lay close behind: during the 1860s, the coach from Whetstone had taken about an hour and three quarters. Living out here and working in London were becoming easier to combine.

The houses along Alexandra Grove grew around a much larger home: Moss Hall, a red-brick country house set in its own grounds. Its estate was divided into building plots from 1867. The hall survived among curving paths and grounds edged with trees while the new street acquired separate addresses.

One was Miss Amner's Moss Hall College for Ladies, listed in a directory of 1886 to 1887. That name opens onto a changing world of girls' education.

In many middle-class girls' schools, music was expected even of pupils with little taste for it. Picture the piano lessons: one girl at the keyboard, then another, each needing individual attention. Hours went into acquiring the accomplishments expected of a young lady.

But that idea of education was being challenged. Some schools were taking mathematics seriously, and in 1878 the University of London opened full degrees to women. Alongside the expectation of an accomplished young lady stood the possibility of a future graduate.

The new suburb was already more than a place to come home to. It was somewhere to spend the day learning.

Continue along Alexandra Grove towards the Ballards Lane junction. Moss Hall Crescent is the small road on the right, running alongside Ballards Lane with a green strip between them. Follow the map and stay on the pavement.

## What the revision can now explain

The railway opening can have a meaningful before-state. There is a recorded earlier coach duration, but its origin differs from the rail example. There is also a cleaner comparison in service availability from the same locality. The housing replaced a country-house estate: the hall and its remaining grounds can be located beside the developing street, without inventing a crop or turning Nether Street's medieval history into Alexandra Grove's land survey.

The school discovery can introduce a genuinely unfamiliar educational world. The promising subject is the tension between accomplishments expected of a young lady and widening academic opportunities. Use an imagined period setting, with the local directory as the entry point. Do not assign an unrecorded curriculum to Miss Amner or assume that all girls' schools shared the same ambitions.

**Comparison boundary:** the duration pair has different origins and unspecified London endpoints. It cannot establish an Alexandra Grove journey time, a same-trip saving, a speedup ratio or a door-to-door commute. The frequency comparison better supports the practical change: more chances to travel and return. Neither proves that every resident commuted.

**Writer choice:** the two duration figures can be presented as explicitly located regional examples, naming Whetstone and Church End. Avoid a construction in which “the journey” quietly becomes the same journey. If explaining that geography consumes the opening, use the service-availability contrast with the historical rail duration instead. Keep the endpoint gap in notes; a spoken research disclaimer adds little.

**Remaining lead, not a dependency:** VCH fn. 109 cites T. C. Barker and Michael Robbins, *A History of London Transport*, vol. 1 (George Allen & Unwin, 1963), p. 170; fn. 105 also cites that page. The [bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_History_of_London_Transport.html?id=FYES0gEACAAJ) was opened, but does not expose p. 170. Its underlying service details could sharpen the London destination. No paid archive, borrowing account or speculative distance/speed calculation was used. A matched pre-1867 Church End duration remains unclosed.

## 3. The local educational evidence

**R02-S03 / existing P02-S03.** *Kelly's Street Directory 1886 to 1887*, Finchley, Friern Barnet, Whetstone and Totteridge streets section; Barnet Local Studies and Archives digitisation, 2010. [Council scan](https://open.barnet.gov.uk/download/2rpm1/m4f/Kelly%27s_Street_Dirctory_Finchley_1886.pdf). Independently downloaded, first PDF page rendered and visually inspected on 20 September.

**P02-C09 confirmed:** Alexandra Grove's right-side column lists historical no. 13, Amner Miss, “Moss Hall college for ladies.” Farther down is Rhoda Gwynne's ladies' school. The directory's layout identifies the street and its two educational entries; it does not establish today's door numbers or surviving premises.

**School type:** a named woman's establishment in a residential directory reasonably suggests a privately conducted institution. That is an inference, not inspected governance or fee evidence. “College” establishes neither university status nor an adult-only age range; “ladies” does not establish finishing-school-only teaching. Ages, boarding, class size, fees, subjects, examination entries and family backgrounds remain unknown. The estate's middle-class residential history makes contemporary middle-class girls' education useful context, but does not prove every pupil's class or household circumstances.

The modern Moss Hall primary schools are not evidence for this institution. Neither national elementary-school attendance rules nor industrial-school domestic training should be silently applied here.

## 4. Revealing period details and contrary evidence

### R02-S04 — a contemporary inquiry into the relevant social setting

Schools Inquiry Commission, *Report*, vol. I (HMSO, 1868), Chapter VI, pp. 546–570; [transcription by Derek Gillard, 2018](https://www.education-uk.org/documents/taunton1868/taunton1.html), independently opened 20 September. A primary official report, mediated by a transcription with declared punctuation/typing corrections; the original scan was not compared. Its scope is principally middle-class education outside state-aided elementary provision. Reformers' judgements and contemporary prejudices require interpretation.

| Claim | Detail available for selection | Locator / status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R02-C06 | Music was commonly expected irrespective of aptitude; pianoforte tuition absorbed time and could not be taught simultaneously to a class. | pp. 551–552; “however little taste”; directly reported criticism. |
| R02-C07 | Ornamental needlework could occupy excessive time; French led modern-language teaching. | pp. 551–552; “ornamental character”; directly reported, not a timetable for every school. |
| R02-C08 | Some schools taught mathematics successfully; the report acknowledged improving provision and exceptions to the music expectation. | pp. 550–553; directly supported counterweight. |

This evidence is about nineteen years earlier than the directory. It supports the educational expectations being challenged, not a frozen description of every school in 1887. Avoid repeating the commissioners' judgements about women's innate abilities as facts.

### R02-S05 — an 1887 example of the tension

Oxford High School GDST, [“Our History”](https://oxfordhigh.gdst.net/who-we-are/our-history/), undated institutional history, timeline entry **1887**, opened 20 September. **R02-C09:** Lucy Soulsby's arrival brought chemistry into the curriculum, alongside an assurance that needlework would retain its place. Minimal passage: “modern demand on girls' brains.” Directly supported as the school's retrospective account; underlying speech not independently inspected.

This is a particular reforming high school, not proof about Alexandra Grove or the average private school. Its value is to show that the tension remained live in the directory's year. If used as an anecdote, name Oxford; never move its chemistry class or headmistress to Moss Hall. Its introductory promotional language and broad claim about universities are not adopted.

### R02-S06 — what academic ambition could mean

University of London, [institutional timeline](https://www.london.ac.uk/about/history), entry **1878**, opened 20 September. **R02-C10:** full degrees were opened to women in 1878. Minimal passage: “open full degrees for women.” Use admission/eligibility, not a claim that degrees were first awarded that year; the page's introductory summary blurs that distinction.

Philip Carter, Institute of Historical Research, [“Oh Pioneers! Remembering the London Nine”](https://www.london.ac.uk/news-events/blogs/oh-pioneers-remembering-london-nine), 30 April 2018, updated 8 November 2024; opened 20 September. **R02-C11:** the earlier 1869 women's examination included substantial academic subjects but led to a certificate, not a degree; candidates could come through schools or private tuition. Locator: “Certificates of Proficiency” and final historical paragraph. Minimal passage: “Certificate of Proficiency.” Authoritative historical interpretation, not inspection of individual candidate papers.

The useful contrast is a recently opened academic possibility, not a claim that Miss Amner prepared university entrants or that access was universal. Oxford and Cambridge had different restrictions; a blanket “women could not go to university” would be false for 1887.

## 5. Recommended combination and reconstruction boundaries

**Provisional promise:** the familiar residential street emerged as both access to London and expectations for everyday life were changing. The writer should test the join rather than force two histories into a single causal claim.

Suggested allocation within approximately **260–285 words including 37 direction words**:

1. **Travel, 50–60 words:** preferred railway-led opening; select the honest regional duration comparison or the cleaner service contrast. Do not use both full explanations.
2. **Landscape, 40–50:** establish a red-brick country house in its grounds and the move to separate building plots. A broad reference to the houses around the walker earns the locality.
3. **School and period scene, 100–115:** introduce one directory entry, then an explicitly imagined setting shaped by C06/C07. The distinctive feature is educational purpose and constraint, not chairs and books. Repeated piano practice despite little aptitude is a plausible reconstructed action; an exact number of hours, instrument in a particular room, pupil's thoughts or Miss Amner's demands would be invented local specifics. Balance the older expectation with C10's recent academic opening.
4. **Return, about 20:** reconnect the wider subject to the activities once housed along this residential street, without proclaiming that this particular college led the reforms.

**Strong alternative:** use Oxford's chemistry/needlework episode instead of the imagined music scene. It is vivid and precisely dated, but requires another named place and may turn the chapter into an anecdotal excursion. **Consequential omissions:** the second local school; station-opening lists; estate lease mechanics; demolition date; university exam questions and the matron anecdote; biographies of reformers. Each competes with developing the selected picture. Keep the Crescent's villa agreements and garden arrangements for its own stop.

## 1. Welcome to Highgate

`highgate-station` · stationary story · 78 words before navigation

Welcome to Highgate. Our walk takes us up into the village, across Hampstead Heath, and on to two homes where people made room for unusually creative lives. There will be stories along the way, and stretches to enjoy the Heath in peace.

Highgate's first railway station opened in eighteen sixty-seven, well before the Tube platforms used today. We'll leave the transport history there: the village is older still, and has a rather more entertaining way of welcoming visitors.

## 2. A very accommodating oath

`pond-square` · stationary story · 183 words before navigation

Pond Square once held the village's drinking-water ponds. They were filled in during the nineteenth century, leaving this space among the houses. Water was one practical necessity in a place that served travellers: Highgate grew around a road through the Bishop of London's land, with a toll payable to the bishop and inns catering to the passing trade.

The inns also offered something less essential: the Highgate oath. Picture the ceremony in a satirical print from seventeen ninety-six. Outside the Horns Tavern, a landlord holds an open book. His customer prepares to swear, while a boy raises a pole topped with horns. A woman appears with a huge foaming tankard, and the onlookers are plainly enjoying themselves.

The visitor must promise to choose white bread over brown, unless he likes brown better. And strong beer over weak, unless he prefers the weak. A solemn ceremony, with an escape clause for every taste. The joke was already being recorded as an old custom in the seventeen-nineties. It is an unusually considerate oath: all that formality, and you can still have the beer you wanted.

## 3. A walk with a very talkative poet

`walking-conversation` · walking passage · 110 words before navigation

Elsewhere on this Heath, near Kenwood, two poets met by chance in April eighteen nineteen. The young John Keats joined the older, celebrated Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who wrote The Ancient Mariner, and one of Keats's former medical teachers.

For nearly two miles, Coleridge walked along at what Keats called an alderman-after-dinner pace: slow walking, with a remarkably busy mind. The conversation ran through nightingales, poetry, dreams and consciousness, then out to the kraken, mermaids and ghosts. Keats's amused report ends with a simple observation. He could hear Coleridge talking as he approached. He could hear him as he went away. And he had heard him all the time in between.

## 4. London’s wooden plumbing

`highgate-ponds` · stationary story · 179 words before navigation

The Highgate ponds have a practical origin that their leafy setting can disguise. This chain was made by damming a stream fed by springs near Kenwood, storing water for London. Each pond connects to the next through pipes or streams. What looks like a series of separate pools is also a linked piece of engineering.

The Hampstead Water Company carried its supply into north London through wooden pipes. Imagine the work hidden inside that phrase: elm trunks with holes bored along their length, turning timber into channels for water. A tree could become part of the city's plumbing. These reservoirs belonged to a system reaching well beyond the people who lived beside them.

The eastern stream eventually joins its western neighbour to form the Fleet, the river now buried beneath London on its way to the Thames. Up here, its beginnings remain open to the sky. The ponds no longer supply drinking water. Swimming is one of their present uses, alongside wildlife and the quieter pleasure of watching the water. Infrastructure has become somewhere people come for a break.

## 5. Keeping room for the Heath

`keeping-the-heath` · walking passage · 164 words before navigation

Keeping the Heath open has involved both buying land and looking after it. An eighteen seventy-one Act secured the original Heath for London's Metropolitan Board of Works; later purchases enlarged it. Today the City of London Corporation, the authority for the Square Mile, holds and manages the Heath as a charity. Its staff and conservation volunteers help decide how that space is kept.

As you walk, notice the changes between open ground, scrub and trees. Much of the woodland spread as farming and grazing declined. Keeping some areas open now means checking that growth. Elsewhere, fallen wood can stay, providing habitat as it decays. A tidy-looking landscape would lose some of what makes this one valuable.

One September experiment uses sheep on an anthill meadow elsewhere on the Heath. The mounds make machines awkward; grazing offers a gentler way to manage the vegetation. Ecologists compare the site before and after. Even leaving room for a walk can involve deciding who should cut the grass.

## 6. A home becomes an exhibition

`willow-road` · stationary story · 216 words before navigation

The long windows and red brick at two Willow Road belong to a home completed in nineteen thirty-nine for architect Ernő Goldfinger and his wife Ursula. It is one of three houses built around a concrete frame. Goldfinger argued that their proportions belonged in the same tradition as Hampstead's Georgian buildings. Modern construction, he thought, could be a good neighbour.

A local preservation campaigner, Henry Brooke, strongly disagreed. He feared the design would damage the neighbourhood's character. The argument makes an entertaining backdrop to what happened inside only a few years later, when this new family home became a venue for wartime fundraising.

Picture the rooms in nineteen forty-two, given over to roughly seventy works of art, many offered at auction to help people in the Soviet Union facing the Nazi invasion. Among the works was a small head by the sculptor Henry Moore: carved elm, its hollows crossed by taut strings. The Goldfingers bought it themselves. This was a home where experimental art could be looked at, argued over, and put to practical use.

Eventually the house itself became something to preserve. When it passed to the National Trust, the Heritage Secretary involved was Peter Brooke, son of its most vocal opponent. Within one family, yesterday's threat to Hampstead had become part of its heritage.

## 7. Something worth sharing

`keats-house` · stationary story · 203 words before navigation

Here is the home of John Keats, the younger poet from our walking conversation. The building began as two separate households designed to look like one, sharing a garden. Keats came to live with his friend Charles Brown in eighteen eighteen, and fell in love with his neighbour Fanny Brawne. The famous address was full of relationships before it became a monument to one writer.

That garden helped inspire Ode to a Nightingale. A bird's song became a poem, and, much later, the poem helped another artist respond to a very personal problem. In nineteen ninety-seven, Martin Huxter was in France with his mother. He could hear nightingales singing. Her hearing aids could not pick them up.

Huxter began trying to convey the song through drawing, using materials including charcoal and ink. His work has come here as The Nightingale Suite, an exhibition running this autumn. The starting point was that small gap between two people in the same place: one hearing something wonderful, the other unable to share it.

It is a generous ambition for a picture, or a poem: to pass something on that another person might otherwise miss. Our walk ends here, with that wish. Thank you for coming along.

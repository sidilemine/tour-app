## K1 — Keats House as shared domestic life

Source: City of London/Keats House, [History of the House](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/attractions-museums-entertainment/keats-house/history-of-the-house), updated 29 May 2026. Custodian's account.

- K1a: William Woods built Wentworth Place in 1814–16 as two semi-detached homes with shared garden, designed to read as one house. Dilke family occupied larger half; Charles Brown the smaller eastern one.
- K1b: Brown invited Keats to share after his brother Tom's death, December 1818. Keats fell in love with neighbour Fanny Brawne; departed September 1820, died in Rome in February 1821. Do not build whole story around death.
- K1c: Actor Eliza Jane Chester joined the homes in 1838 and added an entertaining room. Later naming/alterations defeated William Howitt's 1847 search; 1878 identification and an 1896 plaque followed.
- Most promising selection: visible double-house facade + interdependent household/friends/love, then deliberate preservation/current poetry. Avoid full owner chronology/laundry list. The house is not a preserved lone genius's private estate.

## K2 — Women whose correspondence survived

Source: City of London/Keats House, [The journey of the letters](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/attractions-museums-entertainment/keats-house/letters-fanny-keats-and-fanny-brawne/the-journey-of-the-letters), updated 23 November 2022. Custodian's manuscript description.

- Thirty-one letters from Fanny Brawne to Keats's sister Fanny, four years from 1820; economical cross-hatching, iron-gall ink. Different women; introduce relationship clearly.
- Keats’s sister Fanny married in 1826 and moved into the former Brown/Keats side in 1828, while the Brawnes were still in the other half. Later took family manuscripts personally to Spain; luggage confiscated but these survived. Potential richer optional angle, not obligatory second biographical paragraph.
- Short support: “cross-hatching, a common way to save paper.” No claims about every letter's sensory sound/scene.

## C1 — A walk and an unstoppable conversation

Primary source: John Keats's journal-letter to George and Georgiana, April 1819, in Sidney Colvin's edited [Letters to His Family and Friends](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35698/35698-h/35698-h.htm), pp 243–244; actual passage inspected (April 15 section, preceding later April 16/17 heading). Keats describes the previous Sunday's meeting near Lord Mansfield'spark, with his former medical demonstrator Mr Green and Coleridge. He estimates walking nearly two miles; topics move from nightingales and poetry through dreams and consciousness to the kraken, mermaids and ghosts. His amused final recollection is that Coleridge's voice was audible approaching, throughout, and departing.

Date/localisation cross-check: University of Victoria research project [11April 1819](https://johnkeats.uvic.ca/1819-04-11.html) identifies Mansfield'spark as Kenwood and meeting as11April 1819. This differs from erroneous 1818 or March dates in some public summaries. Do not claim our lowerHeath route is the exact lane or reconstructed footsteps.

Reconstruction opportunity: use the documented conversational movement and gentle humour while actually walking, with one visible landscape anchor; no invented dialogue. Briefly introduce Coleridge as older celebrated poet of Ancient Mariner/Kubla Khan and Keats as young poet with medical training (verify introduced titles/context if used). Explain why this is more than a blue plaque: a living social/intellectual landscape rather than isolated literary homes. Avoid forcing philosophical lesson at every stop. Keats's original letter is public-domain primary writing; edition attribution remains.

## K3 — A present-day way of hearing a poem

City of London, [current visit page](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/attractions-museums-entertainment/keats-house/visit-keats-house) and [artist's exhibition talk](https://newsite.cityoflondon.gov.uk/events/the-nightingale-suite-exhibition-talk), inspected 21 September 2026. ## K5 — A little of the poem itself

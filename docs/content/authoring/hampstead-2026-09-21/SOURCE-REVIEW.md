# Source review — Willow Road, Keats and Coleridge

**Reviewer:** independent heritage/source agent, 21 September 2026. **Reviewed:** [ADDITIONAL-RESEARCH.md](ADDITIONAL-RESEARCH.md) and [BRIEF.md](BRIEF.md), plus the underlying sources below. This is a source/selection review before the prose draft; it does not certify later narration, physical sightlines or access. Research packets were not edited.

## Conclusion

The selected material supports writing now. The strongest combination is Willow Road's contested design and wartime exhibition; Keats's shared house and the present Huxter exhibition; and Keats's own lively account of Coleridge during the walking leg. These give architectural observation, social activity, humour and a warm contemporary ending. No further broad research is needed before drafting.

The only correction found in the packet concerns an **unselected correspondence option**: Fanny Keats married in 1826, moved to Wentworth Place in 1828, and became Fanny Brawne's neighbour then. “Neighbours again” is not established. Keep that optional material out of this already full ending.

## Findings that matter to the selected prose

### 1. Willow construction and the Brooke reversal — supported

The [National Trust construction account](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/london/2-willow-road/the-construction-of-2-willow-road) supports rejected flats/studios in 1936, the final three-house design in 1937, completion in summer 1939, Henry Brooke's opposition and his son Peter's later role as Heritage Secretary at the Trust handover. Its account also explains the concealed concrete frame and Goldfinger's appeal to Georgian proportions.

**Use:** let the listener inspect the frontage before explaining the disagreement. The father/son reversal earns its place as a light ending or transition. Identify Goldfinger as the architect, Henry as a conservation campaigner, Peter as his son and Heritage Secretary. Names without these functions add work for the listener.

**Keep the packet's limits:** no claim that the whole neighbourhood opposed him; no precise handover year while the custodian's accounts differ. Peter's participation does not make him the owner or donor. Do not present the modern house as an exposed concrete box when the material story is largely its concealed structure.

### 2. The 1942 exhibition — enough evidence for a real scene

The Trust's [creative-community account](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/london/2-willow-road/history-of-hampstead-and-2-willow-road) explicitly places the Aid to Russia exhibition at this home in 1942, with approximately seventy works and many offered at auction. The beneficiary was the National Council of Labour's Aid to Russia fund, responding to hardship during the Nazi invasion.

Independent object-level support is strong: the [catalogue for Henry Moore's *Head*, NT 112757](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/112757), records a 1938 sculpture in elm and string, 220 mm high, acquired by Ernő Goldfinger at this exhibition in 1942, catalogue number 65, for 25 guineas. The entry cites the contemporary exhibition catalogue. It supports the named object and transaction, though this review did not inspect a scan of the 1942 catalogue itself.

**Craft opportunity:** a family home temporarily full of modern art, with one small carved wooden head threaded with string. That supplies a distinctive picture the visitor would not get from a generic party vignette. “Sculptor Henry Moore” gives sufficient initial context. No need to add three more artists or the sale price unless the sentence needs them.

**Boundary:** call the exhibition an exhibition, not necessarily a single evening party. Neither source establishes the room in which this object stood, the audience's clothes, particular attendees or a bidding exchange. No implication that the art is visible through the windows today.

### 3. Keats's two-house home — supported; compress the biography

The [City's house history](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/attractions-museums-entertainment/keats-house/history-of-the-house) confirms two semi-detached homes designed to appear as one, a shared garden, Brown inviting Keats into his half in December 1818, and Fanny Brawne living next door. It also records the later joining of the houses in 1838.

**Use:** show the domestic arrangement, introduce Brown as the friend sharing his home and Brawne as the neighbour Keats loved, then move towards the poem. The visible house is a starting point for understanding people living close together, not a promise that all its present features survived unchanged from 1819.

**Avoid:** compressing December 1818 into a claim that Brown, Keats and the Brawnes all moved in together on the same day. The packet does not claim this, but the writer could accidentally imply it. No need to include the later actor, rediscovery, plaques and museum rescue in the same short story.

### 4. Huxter and his mother — retain the warm ending

The [current City visit page](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/attractions-museums-entertainment/keats-house/visit-keats-house), independently reopened in this review, confirms *The Nightingale Suite*, its inspiration in Huxter hearing birdsong and trying to depict it for his mother, and its run through Sunday 1 November. This makes the local/present connection concrete.

The coordinator's [artist-talk source](https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/events/the-nightingale-suite-exhibition-talk) did not reopen successfully during this review. A [syndicated Keats House event listing](https://stayhappening.com/e/the-nightingale-suite-exhibition-talk-E118SB1N9VJMQ) returned the fuller first-person account in search: France in 1997, his mother's hearing aids failing to pick up the song, and his beginning work that evening. This is corroboration of the packet's earlier reading, not a second independent eyewitness. The artist's own site also failed direct retrieval.

**Recommendation:** proceed with the mother/birdsong story. Introduce it as Huxter's account; no need to turn the narration into a source disclaimer. The current custodian page independently establishes its essential meaning. If using the recalled words or precise evening setting, preserve that attribution rather than inventing an omniscient scene. Do not imply she never heard any sound, that all hearing aids fail with birdsong, or that we know how she responded to the completed artwork.

The exhibition's present location supplies the reason to tell this here even when the house is closed. The story should not become a promise to enter today. Avoid inviting the listener to identify a nightingale singing here this September.

### 5. Keats's walking conversation — primary account checked

The [Colvin edition of Keats's letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35698/35698-h/35698-h.htm), p244, contains the meeting with Green and Coleridge beside Mansfield's park, Keats's estimate of nearly two miles, the extraordinary topic list and the joke about hearing Coleridge before, during and after. The [University of Victoria chronology](https://johnkeats.uvic.ca/1819-04-11.html) supports Sunday 11 April 1819 and identifies Mansfield's park with Kenwood.

**Use confidently:** an older, celebrated poet talking at length to a younger poet with medical training; select a few contrasting subjects, then land the joke. “Nearly two miles” remains Keats's estimate, not a measured tour route. The mock-grand “alderman-after-dinner” pace is his own description if a distinctive physical detail is useful.

**Boundary:** no invented dialogue, exact path claim, proof the conversation caused an ode, or later Coleridge death-prediction anecdote. Keats reports joining two people: do not frame the encounter as a prearranged private walk between two poets. Green can remain an unnamed former medical teacher if the extra name would clutter a 70-second chapter.

## Checks for the forthcoming prose review

1. Willow has an observable exterior cue, one clear disagreement and specific activity; it does not become an architectural chronology followed by an artist rollcall.
2. Keats gives enough lived context to make the poetry matter, then Huxter closes the past/present connection warmly. Avoid consecutive endings about preservation, literature, love and the library.
3. The walking conversation sounds like a walk, with humour and breath; it does not require standing still to follow a philosophical lecture.
4. Supported imagination is allowed to do its job. A natural cue into a remembered account or reconstructed exhibition is sufficient; no repeated hedge before every concrete detail.
5. Date/current-opening facts belong in supporting route information unless needed by the story. Each claim's limits can remain in research rather than all becoming spoken caveats.

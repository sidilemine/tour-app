## Ready for writing

The compact trio is viable: **QUEENS → Whiteleys → 23/24 Leinster Gardens**, with one short walking passage about the surrounding residential terraces. They offer different pleasures: a playful archival surprise, a business ambition made architectural, and a visual puzzle with a concrete engineering explanation. Allow the reveal to happen only at the last stop; avoid announcing a theory of deceptive façades at the beginning.

### 1. QUEENS: an unexpected indoor season

**Core claims:** `qe-queens-1930`, `qe-skating-season`, `qe-queens-beach-photo`, `qe-queens-now`, `qe-queens-access`.

The entrance provides the contrast with the much larger activity behind it. The period difference is the ability to buy a skating outing without waiting for a London freeze. Use the museum's interwar context to make that change intelligible, then give the historical photograph a little space: its incongruous beach-party props are a better light moment than invented people falling over. Return to the active venue today.

**Initial text-only photo boundary (superseded by the visual correction below):** the museum identifies the event and supplies a descriptive image alternative. The underlying photograph has not been independently visually inspected in this pass. The source does **not** establish that children were skating in swimming costumes, that the beach set stood on the ice, or that this happened in 1930. Keep those additions out. An external image-shop index suggests July 1952, but the exact photograph match is unconfirmed; omit the date. A writer may frame the detail explicitly as a surviving photograph, without a long sourcing preamble.

No royalty list, skating champion rollcall, BBC-first claim, uncertain founder's name or “oldest/only” superlative is needed. The official homepage's “130 years” wording conflicts with its own 1930 history; use the museum-supported year. Do not promise a free look inside: the current FAQ lists a small spectator admission charge. The stop works outside.

### 2. Whiteleys: the universal provider grows a palace

**Core claims:** `qe-whiteley-origins`, `qe-whiteley-range`, `qe-whiteley-catalogue`, `qe-whiteleys-building`, `qe-whiteleys-move`, `qe-whiteleys-now`.

Give William Whiteley his role immediately: the draper who expanded into a department-store business. An ordinary original shop and an extraordinary commercial ambition provide the narrative movement. A surviving catalogue supplies distinctive things to imagine buying; select two or three examples, rather than reading an inventory. The architectural scale is the visible payoff. The present reuse is a useful final change of purpose, not evidence that a whole neighbourhood has been regenerated successfully.

**Essential chronology:** his original 1863 business was in **Westbourne Grove**. The surviving Queensway store belongs to **1908–12**, and the archive records the move in **1911**. Do not stage the first shop, the early expansions or Whiteley's death inside this later building. The current frontage is stone-faced over a steel frame; “Art Deco” is inappropriate shorthand for this listed Edwardian building despite current hotel marketing using it.

**Reserve, not required:** `qe-whiteley-staff`, `qe-whiteley-legacy`. Staff living arrangements are a researched contrast with the glamour, but introducing a second whole labour narrative can overload a 140–180-word stop. The charitable legacy takes the listener away from the visible place. Prefer a clean store scene unless the writer finds a natural economical use.

**Exclude:** the elephant-delivery story is promoted by the present developer but not established here by contemporary evidence. The murder adds sensational detail and a geographic trap; omit it. In any later use, the alleged son's claimed paternity is not an established fact.

### 3. Leinster Gardens: two addresses, no houses

**Core claims:** `qe-leinster-demolition`, `qe-leinster-screen`, `qe-leinster-steam`, `qe-leinster-ventilation`.

Let the listener look before explaining the trick. The archival construction photograph gives the mechanism: an existing terrace was interrupted by railway construction and a screen maintained its street appearance. Introduce steam underground as a real period difference, then explain the open-air gap's purpose simply. The satisfying ending is the relationship between what the street shows and what had to happen behind it; it need not become a generic moral about appearances.

The strongest sources are a construction-era photograph catalogued by London Museum and the statutory listing. The listing's approximate 1855 date applies to the **terrace group**, not the later screen's construction. Say the railway work happened in the 1860s; do not misidentify its operating company from the photograph collection's broad title.

**Engineering restraint:** ventilation of steam-era railways is supported; the widely copied account of condensers “capturing carboniferous smoke” is not. Steam condensation and coal-smoke removal are different things. No intricate condenser lesson is needed here.

**Physical restraint:** the route reviewer must confirm any instruction involving particular windows, letterboxes or door details. The screen's existence does not establish that every fine detail will be visible in evening light. Do not require seeing over the rear parapet on Porchester Terrace. Do not promise a train will pass or be audible.

**Exclude:** the fraudulent charity-ball invitations, prank deliveries, exact wall thickness and homeowner-pressure motive remain unverified in this pass. The real engineering surprise supplies enough delight.

## Walking chapter: what these terraces were built to do

**Available claims:** `qe-bayswater-development`, `qe-bayswater-terraces`, `qe-bayswater-conversion`.

A supported short passage can explain the nineteenth-century residential expansion westward, the deliberately grand classical street architecture, and the later division of many large houses into flats. Invite attention to repeated architectural forms and individual entrances only where the actual walking corridor supports that view. The architectural grandeur was residential before it became the visitor's present streetscape; that offers context without revealing the false houses in advance.

**Keep the geography honest:** the council guide's 1827 beginning concerns the eastern sector between Praed Street, Edgware Road and Bayswater Road, with expansion westward. It is not a construction date for Queensborough Terrace or a claim that one planner designed every street on this walk. The 2004 guide's account of conversions is a dated area description, not a survey of every building's current use. Current hotel signs can supply a direct observation if route imagery confirms them.

**Not ready to assert:** one developer for the whole area; exact class/servant allocation floor by floor; hotel conversion dates for an individual address; a researched seventy-year/multiple-developer narrative. A larger conservation audit is available but exceeded the browser fetch limit. A shorter official guide was read in full. Those gaps need not hold the three stops or an economical chapter.

## Source and drafting controls

- Source records distinguish institutional history, contemporary objects, statutory listings and present operators. Repeated promotional accounts do not count as independent corroboration.
- Supporting quotations are kept in `claims.json` only; the aggregate is below 25 quoted words for each source page. Source IDs resolve in `sources.json`.
- Claims concern historical content and dated official information. They do not establish pavement space, present construction obstructions, a safe crossing or exterior lighting.
- Reconstruction can connect established facts into a picture, but add no unresearched uniforms, smells, prices, crowd composition, interiors or spoken dialogue. For this very short demo, one precise archival image and one selective catalogue scene do more work than three generic vignettes.
- St Sophia remains a credible alternative, with [the cathedral's own history](https://www.stsophia.org.uk/history/) identifying a Greek commercial community's earlier City worship and later Bayswater church. It is not developed into a fourth stop: three distinct scenes already fit the owner's brief better.

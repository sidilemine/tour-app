## Exact spoken text

### queens-rink — An unlikely season

Welcome to Queensway. This short guide was made with AI, using published research. We'll start at Queens.

Behind the Queens entrance is an ice rink that opened in nineteen thirty, when London was falling for skating indoors. No waiting for a pond to freeze, no hoping a cold spell would last: winter could be booked into an ordinary day on Queensway.

One surviving photograph of a children's party at the club takes that freedom with the seasons a little further. The children are in swimming costumes, gathered around a beach umbrella and a makeshift boat. They've brought the seaside to an ice club.

The party is long over, but the rink remains in use, with public skating and lessons. That modest entrance still opens onto a rather unlikely piece of London: a place where a trip to the high street can end with a turn round the ice.

### whiteleys — The universal provider

Whiteleys gives shopping the frontage of a palace: tall columns, pale stone, a whole stretch of street claimed by one enormous shop. The business began much more modestly. In eighteen sixty-three, William Whiteley opened a small draper's shop on nearby Westbourne Grove.

He expanded into a universal provider. You could buy clothing and food, arrange decorating, even look for a house. A surviving nineteenth-century catalogue adds croquet, archery and polo equipment. Imagine furnishing your leisure time as carefully as your home: a new pastime arriving with the shopping.

The business moved into this Queensway building in nineteen eleven. Behind the old frontage today are homes, commercial spaces and the newly opened Six Senses hotel. The shopping palace has acquired bedrooms.

### leinster-gardens — Leinster Gardens

Take a moment to look along this terrace. Two of its most convincing houses have no rooms at all. Numbers twenty-three and twenty-four are fronts: the street face of a building, with the building missing.

When the Underground was built through here in the eighteen sixties, two houses were demolished. The replacement screen preserved the appearance of the row, but behind it the railway remained open to the sky.

The trains then were hauled by steam locomotives. Underground railways needed places to breathe, and this gap provided ventilation without leaving a hole in the street's grand architecture.

So the houses on either side could keep their respectable neighbours, even though the new occupants were passing trains. The fronts have outlasted the steam engines. A solution to a nineteenth-century engineering problem is still playing its part in the scenery of an ordinary London street.

### bayswater-terraces — A street of grand houses

On these quieter streets, the houses seem to belong to one another. Bayswater grew westwards during the nineteenth century as a fashionable residential district. Classical columns and repeated windows gave its terraces a grandeur that extended beyond any one front door.

Later, many of the larger houses were divided into flats. One substantial house could become several homes, while the street outside kept much of its original shape.

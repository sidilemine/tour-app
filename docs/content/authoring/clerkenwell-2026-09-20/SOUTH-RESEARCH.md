## 1. Smithfield — the day that happened before yours

### Shortlist and proposed selection

1. **Select: the human relay.** The badge record supplies a particular organisation of labour, rather than generic men carrying heavy things (`S-C02`). It gives the listener an unfamiliar picture and vocabulary with a reason behind it. Use at most two job names aloud; explain the actions immediately.
2. **Select: why the clock ran differently.** Supply had to precede customers’ own preparation and sales (`S-C03`). This makes the early pub tradition intelligible without treating workers as a drunken stereotype. It also explains what a daytime visitor is unlikely to see.
3. **Select briefly: what is changing now.** The distinction between occupied trading halls and the museum buildings matters to understanding this very place (`S-C05–07`). A short ending can hold continuity and change together without adopting redevelopment publicity as everyone’s opinion.
4. **Strong alternative: a railway under the dinner.** Basement deliveries and hydraulic lifts make the architecture a working system (`S-C01`, `S-C04`). This could replace some labour detail if the chosen view reads especially well, or support a walking chapter with a clear local return. Do not narrate the full porter system and full engineering explanation twice.

### Reconstruction that would add understanding

Set a mid-twentieth-century frame before imagining the relay in `S-C02`: one load passes through jobs with separate names and separate earnings; the buyer cannot simply treat moving it as an incidental favour. `S-C08` offers barrows and trays as another documented handling method. The useful evocation is the organisation of bodies, goods and payment, not a stock list of cold air, blood, shouting and clattering.

Keep the earlier railway explanation visibly earlier. A seamless imaginary shift that mixes a Victorian meat train, a 1977 badge and modern sealed loading bays would be false chronology. No invented named porter, dialogue, weight, wage, smell or comic incident is needed. The architectural mechanism can be explained literally without manufacturing an eyewitness scene.

### View and present context

The story belongs to the **East/West trading buildings**, so it need not depend on access around the museum’s General Market. An exterior that reveals the market hall or loading frontage is enough; the hidden element is precisely the labour and infrastructure beyond the façade. The route researcher is handling actual standing places and works alternatives. Look for a building-led locator, not a claim that a particular porter or van will be present.

The museum’s long history page uses forward-looking present tense about its home. Prefer the dated opening announcement when stating admission status. The tenants’ older history page still describes the Poultry Market as in use; that wording is stale against the museum project material. Neither ambiguity invalidates the active meat market’s separately listed hours.

## 2. Booth’s panels — production pictured, façade relocated

### What the encounter can do

The attraction is in turning decoration into an intelligible action. First allow a quiet look; then explain the product’s two components—alcohol and botanical flavour—without pretending every operation is recoverable from the carving. End with the survival/reassembly, returning attention to the wall actually in front of the visitor.

**Recommended:** the object plus one process explanation. **Alternative:** make this chiefly a story about architectural salvage if the small actions prove difficult to read from the available pavement. Both earn the same short stop; they should not be expanded into two separate speeches.

### What we can say, infer and picture

The scientific point is compact: the flavour of gin is not simply the flavour of the harvested grain. Botanical material gives a relatively neutral alcohol its characteristic aroma; consistency needs judgement (`S-C13`). This helps a drink become work. It is not necessary to read out a spice cupboard or explain legal categories.

An imagined cart, field or tasting scene should follow the **artwork’s representation** once the route imagery confirms it. The panels celebrate a chain of labour; they are not photographs of Booth employees. Do not infer that fields surrounded this building, that depicted oxen actually delivered the company’s barrels, or that a sculpted figure’s expression records a worker’s opinion. The guide’s colourful facial interpretations remain discovery prose, not historical evidence.

**Locator from documentary evidence:** the five pale carved panels between the upper windows of the striped brick-and-stone frontage at number 25; `S-S08`. Orientation, readable distance and exact incoming approach belong to route review. This research has not visually certified the panels’ present unobstructed state.

### Contradictions resolved or deliberately avoided

- **1740 versus 1770s:** a commonly repeated brand founding date is not a verified date for this distillery’s local occupation. Use the documented local sequence or omit the date.
- **1971 versus 1975 demolition:** secondary descriptions disagree. The useful supported fact is reassembly in 1975–76; exact demolition year adds little and remains out of narration.
- **“Only the granite survived”:** the official account also describes the carved panels and renewal of other material. Avoid a blanket claim about what is original.
- **National listing:** the 2013 record is a decision **not** to list nationally, despite historic interest. It must not be cited as a listed-building entry; local designation is a different question.

## 3. St John’s Gate — Parliament in disguise

### One developed episode

**Provisional promise:** understand an eighteenth-century publishing job by following what happened between political speech and a reader’s page.

The gate first needs a concise introduction as a former priory entrance. Then introduce **Edward Cave, the printer and publisher behind the Gentleman’s Magazine**, and **Samuel Johnson, a working writer later famous for his dictionary**. The chosen moment is early in Johnson’s career, not a procession of already-famous men visiting a monument.

Proposed order:

1. **A building with a second identity:** publishing and printing work happened here; its image also sold the publication (`S-C14`, `S-C15`). The visible arch gives the account a concrete workplace.
2. **A reporting constraint:** Parliament resisted publication of proceedings. Set out that restriction plainly; no legal lecture is necessary (`S-C16`).
3. **The entertaining device:** parliamentary material appears as proceedings in Lilliput, a fictional land familiar from *Gulliver’s Travels*. A real inspected instalment supplies a telling detail: the fiction explicitly acknowledges a parallel British debate that readers wanted (`S-C17`). This is more interesting than calling the disguise secret or foolproof.
4. **The actual labour:** notes, memory, revision and rhetorical composition intervened between a speech and print (`S-C18`). Johnson did not simply transcribe a recording. Leave room for wit in the disguise and a clear distinction between reported substance and composed language.
5. **Return to the gate:** the business gave the architecture a new public identity (`S-C15`). Its association with print is something the visitor can now hold alongside the earlier religious one.

That sequence supplies an unfamiliar production method and an enjoyable episode. It need not culminate in a universal lecture about misinformation. Avoid making present technology comparisons so prominent that the place disappears.

### A genuinely different lighter alternative

**The printers become a cast.** A historical account by John Hawkins, quoted in `S-S17`, says Cave wanted to see Johnson’s friend David Garrick act. A room over the arch was prepared; journeymen printers read the supporting parts in Fielding’s *The Mock Doctor*. Hawkins attributes the story to Cave himself. The Survey also records it, dating it to 1740 (`S-S12`). This is a reported anecdote with a named transmission chain, not an observed performance.

It offers a warm workplace scene and levity: the same workers needed for a publication help make a performance. It could replace the more cerebral parliamentary treatment if the central/northern tour is already heavy on politics and print. It should not automatically follow it, and the tour need not claim this was Garrick’s first public London performance: “public” and exact dating are unnecessary complications. No invented dialogue, audience reaction or theatrical mishap is supported.

### Evidence limits worth retaining

- The well-known “Whig dogs” quotation and dinner-party revelation are omitted. A scholarly source preview flags reliability questions; building the episode on them would add avoidable uncertainty. The inspected magazine page already offers real humour.
- Boswell’s later account of Johnson ceasing the reports for fear of propagating falsehood is available, but the browsed Gutenberg version places it in an editorial note. It is not needed for the proposed ending and should not be smuggled in as a contemporary diary entry.
- Do not say a reporter hid in shadows taking notes: a popular modern introductory page says that, but the primary biographical passage supports a more variable process. No need to embellish the mechanics.
- The southern subject overlaps the northern print lead in medium, not necessarily in story. The coordinator should choose whether both reveal sufficiently different working lives; proximity and fame alone are not reasons to keep both.

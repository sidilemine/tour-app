# Clerkenwell: central and northern story packets

Research date: **20 September 2026**. Prepared for the [active brief](BRIEF.md), [writing brief v4](../WRITING-BRIEF.md) and the owner-selected **Working lives, hidden in plain sight** promise. Selection proposals, not approved scripts or observed enjoyment. George is the intended narrator. Route assembly and visitor positions belong to the separate route research; none of the access or present street conditions below has been field-verified.

## What is ready to select

- **Green: making room to print.** Harry Quelch gives a practical human scale to the international politics associated with this building. A remembered shortage of space is more rewarding than a roll-call of famous visitors. Sufficient evidence for a developed short episode, provided the remembered room is not identified as the surviving Lenin Room.
- **Woodbridge/Sekforde: flowers as skilled work and trading capital.** The archival trail now includes actual handwritten pages, not merely a catalogue. The strongest scene is an investigator's visit to women making three kinds of flowers beneath the chapel. A separate street-selling passage records women conducting their own purchases. Select both only if the distinction between making artificial flowers and selling fresh stock remains clear.
- **Exmouth: today's small businesses, with a brief failed-invention coda.** Present activity is supportable from current operators and the council. The official 1899 report confirms the Pluto lamp's failure and removal. Elaborate vending-machine claims remain less secure; a modern fountain tribute must not be called the original lamp.
- **Additional northern option: Ingersoll's mosaic.** A retained commercial name gives a worthwhile exterior target. Its documented use was a warehouse and showroom. A museum watch supplies a concrete adaptation story, but no source here puts that work inside this building. Treat this as a short additional piece, not a newly discovered craft workshop or a device for filling the duration target.

These packets do not establish a 75–90 minute route. Suggested listening lengths below are editorial proportions only, before drafting or measuring George audio. Retain looking and ordinary walking time without extending weak stories to reach a number.

## 1. Clerkenwell Green — making room to print

### Proposed selection

**Core, roughly 90–120 seconds:** introduce Quelch as the editor of the socialist newspaper *Justice*, and Lenin as a Russian revolutionary editor in exile before the 1917 revolution. The printing enterprise at the Green made its equipment available for *Iskra*. Lenin later remembered what the hospitality cost Quelch in working space: a partitioned corner, desk, shelf and a single chair. The small physical inconvenience provides gentle humour without invented dialogue or a comic caricature. Claims CW-N-C01–03.

The listener's gain is understanding print as practical cooperation: political publishing needed equipment, premises and someone willing to rearrange their working day. This is an interpretation of the episode, not proof of an unnamed compositor's politics. The present library's continuing education programme can close the episode in one sentence (C04).

**Omit:** a complete history of the building; revolutionary visitor lists; an unrelated Lenin-and-Stalin pub anecdote; an interior visit dependency. The room story should belong to Quelch, rather than turning him into an anonymous helper in a Lenin pilgrimage. If the southern Gate story already explains publication, give this episode its own purpose—sharing space and resources—and avoid repeating generic descriptions of printing.

## 2. Woodbridge and Sekforde — making and selling flowers

Survey leads developed: **CW-LC-P20**, **CW-CO-S04**, **CW-CO-S05** in the [area survey](../../survey/clerkenwell-2026-09-20/README.md). The AIM25 description identified the archive. The Booth catalogue led to the actual digitised notebook. They are different levels of evidence.

### Proposed pieces and what each adds

**A. A workroom beneath a chapel — select as the centre, about two minutes.** Begin with the work rather than the founder. On a visit recorded in Booth's inquiry, approximately thirty girls and women were making artificial roses, daisies and cornflowers in the schoolroom beneath Woodbridge Chapel; their own workroom was being enlarged (C05). This makes a place otherwise legible as religious architecture also legible as a workplace. The three flowers supply colour and pleasure without assigning anyone an invented personality.

**B. How a flat petal acquires a curve — select one brief process, not a whole production manual.** Museum curators describe fabric shaped with moulds and a heated ball-ended tool before petals were assembled on covered wire stems (C07). Explicitly frame this as how the trade made such flowers, rather than something the investigator recorded every woman doing that afternoon. It reveals difficult handwork behind a small fashionable object. An assistant's estimate of a two-year training period adds a local measure of skill (C06).

**C. The stock is their capital — useful alternative or adjoining walking chapter.** Fresh-flower street sellers faced perishable stock and needed money to buy the next lot. Groom told the investigator that women now made purchases themselves, under a missionary's supervision, after he had previously bought for them; he said sellers gave women concessions. Advances often supplemented the women's existing capital to buy a cheap larger lot (C08). This records trading knowledge and agency within a supervised scheme. It does not make the mission disappear or establish free choice for every participant.

**D. A labelled box — reserve rather than pile onto A–C.** The museum's surviving packaging links artificial flowers to Sekforde Street and presents their sale as a means of earning a living (C09). It offers a useful object if an image is later licensed or a short reading is wanted. The box's charitable language also opens a careful question about who framed the women's public image. The People’s History Museum supplies a counterweight to uncomplicated benevolence (C10).

**Recommended combination:** A + one B detail at the chapel; use C on a suitable walking leg only if it has a clear verbal reset to **street sellers**. Omit D from the main audio unless it earns a distinct job. The seam joining the pieces is the different skill required to make a flower and to make a living from one—not a claim that every maker previously followed one identical career.

### Crucial distinctions from the actual notebook

- The first catalogue entry calls the interviewee John A. Groom; p117 says he was absent and the interviewer spoke to a young assistant. Cite the speaker accordingly.
- The pasted list of mission workers is not a roll of women employed making flowers. Do not manufacture named factory biographies from it.
- The note about reluctance to train for domestic service concerns the **street sellers** offered another occupation. It cannot be reassigned to factory women resisting artificial-flower work.
- The investigator's favourable judgement of the women's appearance and apparent contentment is his judgement. It cannot establish that all were happy, fairly paid or grateful.
- Institutional department totals do not yield an individual's wage. No verified individual pay, hours, employment contract, named maker’s account or exact room layout has been obtained.
- The catalogue labels notebook B/237 “1889”, but its interview descriptions and the first relevant handwritten date concern **1898**. Do not perpetuate the catalogue-level date mechanically.
- S07 says flower-making began in 1879; S09 says a workshop existed by 1876. Avoid selecting an exact founding year for this activity until resolved. “By the late nineteenth century” is sufficient here.
- Thirty people seen during one visit, twenty–thirty trainees reported by an assistant, later employment figures and the box's accommodation claim describe different populations and dates. They are not interchangeable headcounts.

**Reconstruction boundary:** no invented composite worker, thoughts, dialogue, personal impairment, pride or suffering. Use “disabled women” in the narration; historical institution names may be explained briefly if necessary, not adopted as the narrator's labels. AIM25's tools and samples include material from the later Edgware period: do not silently place them in the earlier Clerkenwell room. Photographs, archival scans and museum images have not been cleared for redistribution in an offline package.

## 3. Exmouth — present pleasures and an overambitious lamp

### Proposed selection

Allow the ending to notice activity visible on the day. The council currently lists weekday food pitches; Space EC1's own account describes a mother-and-daughter shop with a taste for colour, pattern and humour (C12–13). That gives contemporary working people a place in the portrait without claiming continuity with Victorian workers. Keep names contextualised, and avoid reading a directory of recommended businesses.

**Lamp coda, about 45–75 seconds if retained:** a hot-drinks lamp once promised to make a street fitting do more than illuminate the street. The useful documented ending is the vestry's refusal to take over the unsuccessful machine even when its owners offered to convert it into a drinking fountain (C14). The humour lies in that modest official anticlimax. Present descriptions identify it as a drinks dispenser, but the official passage read here does not specify menu, prices or workings (C15). Those details are unnecessary for the coda and should remain out pending stronger verification.

GAIL's says its reclaimed Victorian fountain is a tribute to Pluto (C16). This is an optional present-day echo, not an obligatory purchase or an original survival. Do not ask the listener to find the historic lamp: the primary report says it was removed.

## 4. Additional northward anchor — the Ingersoll mosaic

The route researcher proposed **223–227 St John Street**, at the top of Sekforde Street. This research establishes the building’s commercial identity; the route file must establish a usable exterior view.

**Selectable short piece, around 60–90 seconds:** read the surviving company name as evidence of the watch trade's distribution as well as manufacture. Then give one concrete object transformation: an Ingersoll pocket watch in the Clockmakers' collection had lugs added at twelve and six so that it could take a wrist strap. Its dial distinguishes American-made parts from assembly in England (C18). Explain that this is a surviving example of the brand’s watch, not an object known to have passed through this address.

Alternatively, the larger story can briefly describe earlier Clerkenwell specialists making components for firms elsewhere, using the museum's account of a bow-operated precision turn (C19). Choose one process. Layering lathe work, wristwatch adaptation, mass-production history and Vogue patterns into one short stop would give each too little meaning.

**Value and limitation:** the brand is a visible clue to a less visible trading network. This candidate adds a physical thing to search for and a change in how an everyday object is worn. It does not yet deliver the fully documented retained watchmaking workshop initially hoped for. Retain only if it improves the route and story; do not allocate an arbitrary extra ten minutes to it. No “cheap American watches killed every local artisan” claim has been established here.

### Small addition for the Green → Woodbridge walking chapter

The coordinator's proposed split gives the walking chapter **specialist work and domestic streets**, leaving the Ingersoll stop the pocket-to-wrist object story. That prevents repetition.

**CW-N-C20:** after the Seckford estate's redevelopment, skilled craftspeople occupied its houses, sometimes lodging apprentices or clerks. Many used adapted rooms or workshops behind the houses. Watchmaking, jewellery, precious metals, printing and the book trade all occur in the Survey's account (S17, p77). This supports explaining why ordinary-looking houses can belong to an industrial landscape. It does not identify the use of every house passed.

For a proposed 75–100 second chapter, combine C20 with C19's **component specialisation**: the person making a part need not be the firm selling the finished watch. Use the bow-operated precision tool as the one process the listener can picture. Then return attention to the streets before the chapel arrival. The intellectual join is an editorial inference: a compact object can represent work divided among people and premises. No source here traces one particular watch from door to door, so do not narrate such a journey. The flower workroom then offers a distinct, directly observed example of labour occupying unexpected premises.

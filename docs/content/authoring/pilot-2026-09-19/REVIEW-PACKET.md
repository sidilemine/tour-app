# Two tour samples for your editorial review

19 September 2026. We have run the small agent pilot: researchers prepared evidence and route constraints, a writer drafted these two excerpts, and a fresh reviewer checked their sources and telling. This is a desk workshop using familiar Finchley material. The two excerpts are independent; the next complete tour and its area remain for us to choose.

Read the spoken passages first. The short alternatives underneath make the editorial decisions concrete. My proposed starting choices are the warmer artsdepot ending and the street-led walking opening, but neither is selected for you.

## Artsdepot and room to take part

**Setting:** outside artsdepot near Nether Street. **Length:** 317 words. [George's recording](audio/artsdepot-george.mp3) measures 123.650 seconds (about 2:04). The earlier writing estimate was 2:11–2:21 before extra pauses; measured delivery supersedes it for this recording.

Here on Nether Street, artsdepot occupies the site of the Gaumont cinema, which opened in 1937.

Picture its auditorium before a film. Elaborate ceiling lights shine above mahogany wall panels; conversation settles as the lights dim. There was a café too, and a stage equipped for live performance. A night here could mean more than the picture on the screen. The cinema closed in 1980, and the building came down seven years later.

The idea that eventually took its place began with a request for more room. At a local consultation in 1996, people asked for a larger version of the Bull Arts Centre in High Barnet. They already had an example of what an arts centre could offer. Getting this one built took another eight years.

Just before artsdepot opened in 2004, its director, Alison Duthie, explained the plan to the Finchley Society. Visiting theatre productions would share the building with space for educational and local groups. There would be exhibitions, workshops and courses. The café and bar, she hoped, would make it a place to meet. The ambition extended from the special evening out to the activities that could bring people back week after week.

That is easier to picture through one small part of today's programme. Creative Circle is a writing group for people over fifty-five, developing poetry and prose together. Earlier this year, artsdepot advertised a performance made by members of that group, its Dance Company and other local artists. Work developed together had a place on the programme alongside visiting shows.

A performance needs an audience, of course. But the rooms between performances matter too: somewhere to try a line of poetry, hear what someone else has made, and return with another draft. From this pavement, the building holds all those possibilities out of sight. An evening out can begin with somebody else's story and lead to a place for your own.

### A more concrete ending

Replace the final paragraph with:

A theatre gives you somewhere to sit and watch. Here, the writing group also has somewhere to bring the next draft, and the programme has room for work made by its participants. The plan Alison Duthie described in 2004 becomes quite tangible: a building with room for the rehearsal as well as the performance.

**Your choice:** keep the warmer ending about finding room for your own story, use this more concrete ending, or suggest the balance you would prefer. The first leaves an imaginative possibility; the second explains the building's practical function more directly.

## A walking chapter on Alexandra Grove

**Setting:** after leaving the Quaker Meeting House, before the Moss Hall Crescent turn. **Length:** 266 words including the candidate orientation below. [Emma's recording](audio/alexandra-grove-emma.mp3) measures 97.650 seconds (about 1:38), shorter than the 1:54–1:58 writing estimate and proposed 115–125-second target. This is measured desk audio; walking suitability remains unverified.

As you walk along Alexandra Grove, the separate front doors make this look like an ordinary residential street. In the nineteenth century, this neighbourhood was taking shape around one much larger home: Moss Hall.

From 1867, its estate was divided into development plots, chiefly for middle-class housing. The railway was making London easier to reach, and the land could become a series of separate building projects. Nearby Dale and Hutton Groves were developed through building leases: permission to build on land held under a lease. The country house stayed while its former estate acquired streets and new inhabitants. Moss Hall itself survived until 1927. The suburb arrived long before the old house left.

A street directory from 1886 to 1887 gives those new addresses a little life. Here on Alexandra Grove it lists Miss Amner's Moss Hall College for Ladies. Imagine a room being readied for lessons: books opened on a table, chairs drawn up, then the beginning of conversation. Among the homes were places where people taught and learned. The name Moss Hall could now belong to an educational address as well as the old house.

Behind the separate front doors, a neighbourhood was acquiring its daily routines. A lesson to prepare, a room to open, someone expected at a particular address: the practical life of a street. The big estate was becoming a collection of smaller destinations.

### Candidate orientation

Continue along Alexandra Grove towards the Ballards Lane junction. Moss Hall Crescent is the small road on the right, running alongside Ballards Lane with a green strip between them. Follow the map and stay on the pavement.

This wording is included in the writing budget. The street relationship is supported by the saved map and your report; the exact pedestrian entry remains unresolved. It is ready for prose discussion, with route work still needed before a revised direction is installed.

### A railway opening

Replace the first paragraph with:

By 1870, a train journey from Finchley's Church End into London took half an hour. Imagine fitting that new connection into a day that began and ended at home. Around Alexandra Grove, homes were taking shape on the estate of a country house called Moss Hall.

With the remaining paragraphs and orientation unchanged, this version totals 278 words. Moss Hall is introduced before the next paragraph refers to its estate. Timing remains an estimate, roughly 119–124 seconds before extra pauses.

**Your choice:** start with the street and its old school, or open through the everyday possibility of a train journey into London. I favour the street-led version for its connection to where the listener is walking. A fuller commuting story would be a separate development choice.

Those discoveries changed the material available to the writer.

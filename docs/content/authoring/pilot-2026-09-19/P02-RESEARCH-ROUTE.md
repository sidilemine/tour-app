## Pitch and worthwhile alternative

**Recommended pitch: a suburb arrived before the country house left.** Explain the change of scale from estate to separate development plots, then use one small contemporary record to give the resulting streets life. Alexandra Grove's 1886–87 directory includes Miss Amner's Moss Hall college for ladies and Miss Rhoda Gwynne's ladies' school. The new neighbourhood contained places to learn and work as well as somewhere to come home. That is firmer material for imagined daily life than an unspecified household moving furniture.

The mechanism is modest but supported: land subdivision and building leases allowed development in stages; the original house did not have to disappear first. The sources do **not** establish who organised every development or that residents collectively built the district. A building lease is an agreement allowing development on leased land; the local parties and financial terms have not been recovered. Keep that explanation short and do not turn it into a lesson about modern leasehold law.

**Moment worth keeping:** the street directory puts teaching behind the residential addresses. Use one institution, contextualised in plain English, rather than reciting both proprietors. A schoolbook or a room being prepared for lessons can make the change imaginable without inventing a pupil, dialogue or a documented school day.

**Alternative: a railway changes the shape of an ordinary day.** The railway made London more accessible: the VCH records a half-hour journey from Church End in 1870 and twenty trains each way daily. This is a more tangible explanation of the attraction of suburban housing than three station-opening dates. Develop leaving home, reaching a station and returning as explicitly imagined routine. The half hour is a historical rail journey, not a door-to-door promise or a claim that every resident commuted. West Finchley station's much later opening makes a useful correction to the modern mental map, but is optional.

**Tradeoff:** the preferred pitch is more particular to the street being walked and gives ordinary life a local record. The alternative gives transport a clearer causal role but could fit many London suburbs. At this leg's budget, choose one as the developed middle; the railway can be a brief enabling context in the first. Sidi has not selected either.

## Reconstruction: useful scope and limits

- **Preferred, 1880s:** frame a short imagined moment of preparing for lessons in one of the street's schools. Pages being opened, a table/room readied and voices beginning are ordinary inferred atmosphere. C09 supplies the local activity; none is a recovered scene. Avoid uniforms, bells, subject matter, pupil ages, named pupils or a claim that pupils arrived along this exact pavement.
- **Estate transition:** new brickwork or an unfinished plot may express staged development, but no particular construction site or year on Alexandra Grove has been established. This is less distinctive than the school detail. Do not stack two full scenes.
- **Railway alternative:** a household's morning departure and evening return can illustrate the new connection. Railway dates/times are documented; a resident's occupation, timetable and feelings would be reconstructed. No audible train is promised here.
- **Present return:** use the street's continuing residential character and the destination's house-front/green relationship. Do not claim a visible tree, boundary or house belonged to the old hall, or date the buildings by appearance. No invented nostalgic verdict about a vanished harmonious community.

## Sample A A name that would fit on a postcard

**Place:** Tally Ho Corner. **Tone:** conversational, lightly amused. **Purpose:** connect a familiar name to the everyday problem of arranging to meet someone. **Delivery:** stationary at an eventual checked public viewpoint of the junction; no road crossing or orientation instruction is included.

### Narration A

Tally Ho is quite a name for a road junction. There are competing stories about where it came from, including one involving a coaching company. But a Victorian cyclist offered a wonderfully practical explanation: his friend needed something shorter to write on a postcard.

In an account published in 1880, a writer calling himself Le Voyageur described meeting a friend here for bicycle rides. They arrived by different roads, and kept having to refer to the same junction. His friend eventually tired of writing “Junction of North and Finchley roads” and came up with Tally Ho Corner.

According to the writer, the name spread through cycling notices, and a local hotel put it above the door.

That account doesn't settle who used the name first. But it gives this junction a rather familiar role: the place where you wait for the person coming from the other direction. Next time you arrange to meet at Tally Ho, you'll have something in common with that impatient postcard writer.

## Sample B The street beneath the car park

**Place:** John Parr memorial bench area, edge of Lodge Lane car park. **Tone:** quiet and concrete, without a dramatic voice or music. **Purpose:** connect remembrance to an ordinary neighbourhood and a street no longer present. **Delivery:** stationary, with the bench and car park visible from a checked public standing area. Sitting is optional and must not be promised.

### Narration B

There used to be another street here. William Street ran through what is now Lodge Lane car park.

Barnet Council records that, across William Street, Albert Street and Lodge Lane, there were 114 houses from which 111 men went to fight in the First World War. At least nine were killed.

One of the men remembered here is John Parr, who lived on Lodge Lane. He was seventeen when he died in Belgium in August 1914, and is believed to have been the first British soldier killed in action on the Western Front.

The bench is a recent addition to his story. The council installed it in 2025 after a soldier silhouette commemorating him was stolen.

Parr's name gives us one person to follow. The figures for these three streets give us a sense of the neighbourhood around him. Looking across the car park, there's room to picture the missing street: houses, front doors, neighbours. Those are the surroundings this memorial asks us to remember.

## Required candidate correction

The older War Memorials Online record describes a silhouette and contains an inconsistent death date. Barnet's 2025 notice records its theft and replacement with a bench. At draft 1, the bench replaced the silhouette as the desk-research candidate; after editorial review the stop is not retained. If the material is ever reconsidered, do not script a visible silhouette or direct the listener to a resident's doorway. The candidate pool is updated accordingly.

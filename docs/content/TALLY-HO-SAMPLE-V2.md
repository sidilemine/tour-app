# Tally Ho sample revision 2

16 September 2026. Revised after Sidi's ten comments on the [first sample pair](NORTH-FINCHLEY-SAMPLE-STOPS.md). [Word copy for comments](tally-ho-sample-review-v2.docx). This is a writing proposal, not an accepted stop or a walk-ready script.

**Purpose:** explain how this junction became a stopping and meeting place, then enrich that history with the postcard anecdote. **Tone:** grounded, conversational and lightly amused. **Delivery proposal:** stationary at a useful public view of the junction; visitor position and orientation remain unverified.

## Narration

Tally Ho Corner grew out of two roads meeting. Ballards Lane was a medieval road, but for centuries it stopped short of the High Road. The connection came in 1756, when the lane was extended to join what was then the Great North Road. North Finchley had become a junction on the road out of London.

In the following century, a new toll road from Marylebone joined this route through Ballards Lane. Long-distance travellers needed more than a road: their coaches needed fresh horses. Barnet's local history records sixteen horses kept near this corner by the Tally Ho Coach Company, ready for the Birmingham coach's first change.

That gives Tally Ho a coaching connection. But a Victorian cyclist offered a different account of the name, involving a friend with a postcard to write.

Writing in 1880 as Le Voyageur, he described meeting that friend here for bicycle rides. They came by different roads, so this was a convenient rendezvous. Describing it every time was less convenient. His friend grew tired of writing “Junction of North and Finchley roads” and came up with Tally Ho Corner instead.

According to the writer, cycling notices spread the name, and a local hotel put it above the door. His account doesn't settle who used it first. But it's a pleasingly practical contribution to local history: two people arranging a bicycle ride, and one of them deciding that their meeting place needed a shorter address.

## Response to the comments

- **0 and 9 — context and preference.** Develop Tally Ho. Roads and horse changes now precede the anecdote. A coaching operation near the corner is supported by the council's local history; an identified coaching inn is not established by these sources. Do not turn that suggested anchor into a proven building or pretend the rival naming accounts agree. No Roman origin is claimed. A contemporary coda is optional and should earn its place; none is added here.
- **1 — unexplained numbers.** Omit statistics that lack a useful comparison or narrative purpose. Do not tell the listener how to feel. The horse count here describes the scale of a specific operation, without calling it unusually large.
- **2 — who is commemorated.** The original wording implied a group memorial. If John Parr material is ever reused, distinguish him from the wider neighbourhood cohort: one of those men is commemorated here.
- **3 — theft and replacement.** Keep maintenance and replacement history in the asset/source record unless it materially serves the story. Its factual availability did not justify its inclusion in narration.
- **4 — bring the scene to life, clarified in conversation.** Sidi wants strongly supported imagined colour: the narrator should supply the scene's character and atmosphere, rather than leave the listener to imagine missing buildings unaided. My initial interpretation was too restrictive. A well-supported reconstruction can use ordinary descriptive detail without an eyewitness source for every element; see the living guidance, revision 3.
- **5 — memorial meaning.** The original closing line assigned the memorial an unsupported purpose. Withdraw that interpretation. This is separate from the welcome use of imaginative reconstruction to evoke a vanished place.
- **6 — solemn subjects.** Avoid minor solemn sites in the intended enjoyable walk. If a major attraction merits inclusion, use formal, restrained, conventional treatment without poetic embellishment. This does not rule out reflection in other stories.
- **7 and 8 — focus.** Give each stop one understandable purpose. Do not drift among biography, general history and local colour just because all are sourced. The John Parr sample is retired from the proposed walk, rather than polished into another version.

The [living guidance](TOUR-DESIGN-GUIDANCE.md) and M2 candidate record reflect these lessons. Original Word files and comments are preserved. The later conversation clarification above supersedes the narrower evocation summary in the dated revision 2 Word copy; the Tally Ho narration is unchanged. This revision remains subject to Sidi's writing judgement; acceptance of the direction is not acceptance of every sentence.

Minimal excerpts: “important medieval thoroughfare”; “In 1756 a raised way”. Supports paragraph 1's medieval lane, former disconnection, date and connection to the Great North Road. **Status:** source_checked local-history account. Exact medieval construction date and Roman origins are not asserted.

Barnet Council, *Tally Ho Corner and the High Road (Finchley N12)*, undated [pocket history](https://www.barnet.gov.uk/libraries-old/local-studies-and-archives/pocket-histories/finchley-friern-barnet-and-totteridge-13), paragraph 2, independently gives the 1756 extension. Relevant text was inspected in the search index; direct opening returned HTTP 403. This corroborates the published account, not its archival independence.

### R2 The Marylebone connection

Lynn Radnedge, *Finchley's Toll Gate*, St Mary at Finchley, 27 September 2024, [article](https://stmaryatfinchley.org.uk/finchleys-toll-gate/), opening historical paragraphs. Minimal excerpts: “between Marylebone and Finchley”; “followed the old Ballards Lane”. Supports paragraph 2's new toll route and connection. The Finchley Society PDF, page 1, Regents Park Road section, also records the authorising Act in 1826. **Status:** source_checked local-history accounts. Script uses the century rather than adding another exact date or a completion-date claim. Road development is context, not proof it caused a particular coach service to choose this corner.

### R3 Coach horses

Barnet Council pocket history linked in R1, paragraph 2. Minimal excerpts: “kept 16 horses near the corner”; “the first change of horses for the Birmingham coach”. Supports paragraph 2's company, count, approximate location and purpose. **Status:** source_checked, explicitly attributed in narration. No exact stable footprint, named inn, surviving building or proven origin of the junction name is claimed. The source places the operation in the 1820s and 1830s; that range is omitted from speech to reduce date density.

The [Londonist investigation](https://londonist.com/london/history/how-finchley-s-tally-ho-corner-got-its-name), Laura Reynolds with later additions by Matt Brown, updated 13 February 2025, discusses contemporary coach references and competing locations. Its reminiscence locates a horse change at “either Finchley or Whetstone”; the article's inference is more specific than that passage alone proves. The council account strengthens the local anchor while exact-site and first-naming questions remain unresolved.

North Finchley Town Centre, *Tally-ho! – The truth behind the name*, undated [page](https://northfinchleytowncentre.co.uk/local-stories/story/tally-ho-the-truth-behind-the-name/), reproduced account attributed to Le Voyageur, *The Country Gentleman*, 4 December 1880. Supports paragraphs 3–5's attributed anecdote, publication year, meeting arrangements, cycling notices and hotel sign. **Status:** source_checked as a reproduced account; the original printed issue was not independently inspected. Minimal passages and prior origin evidence remain in [draft 1](NORTH-FINCHLEY-SAMPLE-STOPS.md#a1-the-cyclist-account). The six-word junction description is the only quotation in this narration. The closing assessment is editorial appreciation of the anecdote, not a claim about the memorial, the present hotel or a settled origin.

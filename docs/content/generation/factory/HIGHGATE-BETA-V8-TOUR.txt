2. 3 The Grove: a writer within a household
Measured recording: 75.2 seconds.

Here at number 3 The Grove, the name to keep in mind is Coleridge. Our literary-history source places him here through another name: Gillman. The Gillmans were his hosts, and in November 1823 he moved with their household to this address. This was a move within his Highgate life, not his first arrival in the neighbourhood.

That phrase, “with their household”, is worth dwelling on. A famous resident can make everyone around them disappear from a place’s story. 4. Waterlow Park: grounds given to the public
Measured recording: 75.4 seconds.

The statue here gives us the name Sydney Waterlow. In 1889, he presented Lauderdale House and its grounds to the London County Council. That is the gift we are considering at this stop—not a date for the park’s opening, but a transfer of the house and grounds.

The council was the public body receiving the gift. Put simply, a house and its private grounds were being placed in public hands. Lauderdale House, our next stop, belongs to the same story: the gift joined a building to the landscape around it.

For this story, the path under your feet matters as much as the statue. A monument concentrates our attention on a person; the surrounding park asks us to think about what other people can use. A gift of land can be understood through the ordinary opportunity to walk, pause and spend time outdoors.

Look from the statue towards the sloping grounds. The change we’re considering is not only one of ownership on paper, but of what a landscape can mean to people without a home inside it. At Jacksons Lane, residents asked for a space. Here, the story turns on a gift. Both invite us to notice how places become shared.

5. Lauderdale House: what an exterior cannot tell you
Measured recording: 76 seconds.

Lauderdale House was part of Waterlow’s 1889 gift, together with its grounds. It also carries a much older layer: Historic England records surviving sixteenth-century fabric in the house’s southeast range. “Range” here means a section of the building, not the whole house.

That does not mean the entire exterior before you is sixteenth century, or that the old fabric is exposed for us to recognise from this path. The useful distinction is between what survives within a building and what its present appearance allows us to see.

At The Flask, we considered a building with more than one age. Here, too, it helps to resist the idea of an untouched survivor. An exterior is a meeting point between a place and the present day, not a complete account of everything that remains inside its fabric.
